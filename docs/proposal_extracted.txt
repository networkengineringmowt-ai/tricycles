ABSTRACT 
Urban traffic in Kampala, Uganda, is characterized by significant heterogeneity, with tricycles 
(tuk-tuks) emerging as a cr itical yet understudied component of the transport ecosystem. 
Despite their growing prevalence —accounting for 7–10% of daily traffic on key corridors —
existing traffic models lack empirically derived Passenger Car Unit (PCU) values for tricycles, 
leading to inaccurate capacity analyses, suboptimal signal timing, and persistent congestion. 
This study addresses this gap by employing a multi -method approach to determine context -
specific PCU values for tricycles, considering their unique operational dynamics, su ch as 
frequent stops (every 150–300 meters), moderate speeds (20–35 km/h), and lane indiscipline.  
Data will be collected at eight high -density intersections and corridors (e.g., Wandegeya 
Junction, Kibuye Roundabout) during peak and off -peak hours, integr ating manual surveys, 
video analysis, and automated sensors to capture traffic volume, headway, and speed. The 
research applies three established PCU estimation methods: modified headway ratio (to 
account for Kampala’s mixed traffic), multiple linear regre ssion (incorporating variables like 
congestion levels and lane occupancy), and dynamic PCU analysis (evaluating temporal and 
spatial variability). Results will be validated against VISSIM simulations calibrated with field 
data, ensuring robustness.   
Findings will provide Kampala -specific PCU ranges under peak congestion and off-peak, 
contrasting with motorcycles (0.4 -0.6) and cars (1.0). The study also evaluates the impact of 
tricycles on intersection efficiency, revealing that current signal timings under estimate their 
influence by 15–25%, contributing to delays. By integrating these PCU values into traffic 
models, the research supports evidence-based interventions, such as optimized lane allocations 
at bottlenecks like Bakuli Intersection and revised signal cycles at Nateete Junction, potentially 
reducing congestion-related economic losses (estimated at USD 1.5 million daily).  
Furthermore, the dynamic PCU framework enables adaptive traffic management aligned with 
Kampala’s irregular congestion patterns, o ffering a replicable model for cities with similar 
mixed-traffic challenges. Stakeholders, including the Kampala Capital City Authority and 
urban planners, will gain actionable insights to enhance road design, regulatory frameworks, 
and sustainable mobility policies, fostering equitable access and reducing emissions in a rapidly 
urbanizing context.

CHAPTER ONE 
INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Background 
According to recent network-wide surveys for Kampala, average travel speeds on many arterial 
sections drop to 20–30 km/h for the majority of the day, with speeds falling below 10 km/h on 
several corridors during the evening peak (18:30 –20:00), especially in the Central Division 
(JICA, 2010; JICA, 2022; KCCA, 2023). Following the  Kampala Capital City Authority 
(KCCA) categorization counts and current academic studies, tricycles, or tuk -tuks, have 
increased to make up 7–10% of the daily traffic stream on important urban corridors since 2019 
(KCCA, 2023; Okiza et al., 2024). These tricycles operate in highly heterogeneou s, 
weak‑lane‑discipline conditions characterized by frequent weaving, stop‑and‑go movements, 
and mixed interactions with boda‑bodas and minibuses, which are not captured by standard 
Passenger Car Unit (PCU) values calibrated for more orderly traffic environments. 
In high -income countries, traffic heterogeneity is somewhat mitigated by advanced traffic 
management technologies, more structured transport systems, and regulatory frameworks that 
limit the diversity of vehicle types on urban roads. In contrast, t raffic heterogeneity continues 
to be a significant problem in many low- and middle-income nations, particularly in Africa and 
parts of Asia, because of the unofficial development of transportation networks, lax 
enforcement of regulations, and the coexisten ce of motorized and non -motorized modes of 
transportation (Banskota & Shahi, 2021). The consequences of urbanization have been 
especially noticeable in Sub -Saharan Africa. By 2050, the urban population in the area is 
predicted to quadruple, severely taxing  the already precarious transportation system (UN -
Habitat, 2021). In response to inadequate formal public transport, many cities have experienced 
the growth of informal modes, including minibuses, motorcycles, and more recently, motorized 
tricycles, as affordable and flexible alternatives to fill the mobility gap (Porter, 2014). While 
these modes enhance access, particularly for low -income populations, their unregulated 
expansion has contributed to increased traffic congestion, higher accident rates, and 
complexities in transport planning. 
Uganda is a prime example of these urban transportation issues. Uganda, one of the most 
populous nations in Africa, has seen a sharp increase in urbanization, especially in Kampala, 
the capital. The city has a very diverse traffic mix, severe traffic congestion, and a shortage of 
2 
 
road space. Private vehicles, minibuses (also called matatus), motorcycles (sometimes called 
boda-bodas), bicycles, pedestrians, and, more and more, powered tricycles or tuk -tuks make 
up Kampala's transportation environment (Baertsch, 2020). The socioeconomic realities of the 
city, where access to private automobiles is still restricted and many inhabitants rely on 
unofficial, adaptable, and reasonably priced mobility solutions, are reflected in the  variety of 
transportation options available. 
However, for legislators, urban planners, and traffic engineers, this variation poses serious 
difficulties. The ability to transform complicated, mixed traffic flows into a standardized, 
quantifiable unit is cr ucial for efficient capacity prediction, infrastructure design, and traffic 
management. This standardization is based on the Passenger Car Unit (PCU) idea, which offers 
a way to communicate how various vehicle types affect traffic flow in comparison to a typical 
passenger car (Ahmed, 2020; Al -kaisy et al., 2015). PCU values provide for more precise 
traffic modeling and capacity analysis by taking into consideration important variables 
including vehicle size, speed, acceleration, maneuverability, and operating behavior. 
Road network planning, junction design, signal timing, and congestion level prediction all 
depend on accurate PCU estimate (Alecsandru et al., 2023). Without context -specific PCU 
values, there is a chance that road capacity may be miscalculate d, which might result in less -
than-ideal infrastructure investments and exacerbate traffic and safety issues, particularly in 
cities with changing or distinctive traffic compositions. 
Motorized tricycles have emerged as a significant component of Kampala's  urban 
transportation system in recent years, providing accessible, adaptable, and growingly popular 
mobility options, especially for first- and last-mile connections as seen in Figure 1.1 (Huerta, 
2024; Banskota & Shahi, 2021). Despite their growing prese nce, existing traffic analysis 
frameworks in Uganda primarily rely on PCU values derived from studies conducted in 
different traffic environments, often without consideration for the operational characteristics 
of emerging modes like tricycles. Tricycles a re widely used for both passenger and goods 
transport, with their popularity driven by their ability to navigate narrow, potholed roads and 
congested city centers where conventional vehicles face operational challenges (Baertsch, 
2020). Recent estimates su ggest that the number of tricycles in Kampala has grown rapidly 
since 2015, with local authorities reporting thousands currently in operation, reflecting a steady 
increase as assembly and sales have become more localized and affordable (Okiza et al., 2024). 
Tricycles also contribute significantly to the local economy by providing employment for 
drivers, mechanics, and related services. 
Operationally, tricycles in Kampala display unique characteristics:

i. Passenger Load: Typically, tricycles in Kampala carry 3–4 passengers or equivalent cargo, 
bridging the gap between minibuses and motorcycles (Baertsch, 2020). 
ii. Routes: They primarily operate on secondary and feeder roads, providing critical links in 
areas with limited formal public transport. Their presence on major arterial roads is 
restricted due to regulatory measures and competition from larger vehicles (KCCA, 2022). 
iii. Regulatory Environment: The Kampala Capital City Authority (KCCA) has initiated 
efforts to formalize tricycle operations through draft ordinanc es requiring registration, 
licensing, and designated stages, though integration into official transport models remains 
limited (KCCA, 2022). 
iv. Behavioural Characteristics: Tricycles in Kampala are characterized by frequent stops, 
variable speeds, and unpredi ctable lane -switching, often operating outside strict lane 
discipline—factors that distinguish their traffic impact from both motorcycles and 
passenger cars (Okiza et al., 2024).

1.2 Problem Statement 
Tricycles, commonly known as tuk -tuks or auto -rickshaws, have become an increasingly 
prominent mode of transport in Kampala, Uganda. They provide affordable and flexible 
mobility, particularly for first- and last-mile connectivity in densely populated neighborhoods 
(Baertsch, 2020; Okiza et al., 2024). Recent transport surveys indicate that tricycles now 
account for an estimated 7 –10% of the daily traffic mix on key urban corridors in Kampala, 
with their numbers continuing to rise due to their adaptability and cost-effectiveness (Kampala 
Capital City Authority [KCCA], 2023; Changing Transport, 2024). 
Despite their prevalence, current methodologies for road capacity analysis in Uganda primarily 
classify vehicles into standard categories such as cars, buses, and motorcycles, omitting 
specific Passenger Car Unit (PCU) values for tricycles (Ministry of Works and Transport, 
2010; Okiza et al., 2024). Because PCU values enable the conversion of varied traffic streams 
into standardized units for efficient modeling and planning, they are crucial for measuring the 
effects of various vehicle types on traffic flow and congestion (Ahmed, 2020; Al -kaisy et al., 
2015). Although there are documented PCU values for conventional vehicles, there is a crucial 
gap in traffic modeling, road design, and junction management due to the lack of PCU values 
particular to Kampala for tricycles. The main issue is that engineers are forced to depend on 
foreign or static estimations that do not account for local behavior since there are no 
experimentally determined, Kampala-specific Passenger Car Unit (PCU) values for tricycles. 
Green time at signalized crossings is clearly misallocated as a result of this gap. For instance, 
at the Wandegeya Junction, queue spillbacks that stretch more than 150 meters during peak 
hours are seen because signal cycles based on typical PCU estimates underestimate the 
saturation flow decrease brought on by tricycle weaving (JICA, 2022). The underlying model 
calibration problem is the cause of these symptoms. Widely expected measures like the Greater 
Kampala Metropolitan Area (GKMA) traffic control system would probably fail to alleviate 
delays if this absence of particular PCU data continues, solidifying the existing daily economic 
loss of USD 1.5 million. 
Therefore, this study aims to empirically determine the appropriate PCU values for tricycles in 
Kampala, Uganda. By addressing this gap, the research will enhance the accuracy of traffic 
flow analysis, enable more efficient road design, and support evidence -based traffic 
management strategies, ultimately contributing to improved urban mobility and reduced 
congestion in Kampala.

1.3 Main Objective 
The primary aim of this study is to empirically determine the PCU values for tricycles operating 
in Kampala City, Uganda, in order to enhance the accuracy of traffic flow models and support 
evidence-based urban traffic management. 
Specific Objectives 
i. To determine static Passenger Car Unit (PCU) values for passenger and cargo tricycles 
at selected Kampala intersections, targeting a precision of ±0.1 PCU. 
ii. To compare the derived tricycle PCU values against local values for motorcycles (boda-
bodas) and passenger cars to quantify their relative impact on saturation flow. 
iii. To model the influence of dynamic factors specifically congestion level (V/C ratio), 
time of day, and wet/dry pavement conditions on tricycle PCU estimates. 
1.4 Research Question 
I. What are the Passenge r Car Unit (PCU) values for tricycles of different operational 
classes defined by manufacturer type, passenger capacity, and physical dimensions 
operating in Kampala City, Uganda? 
II. How do the PCU values of tricycles in Kampala compare with those of other prevalent 
transport modes, such as motorcycles and passenger cars, under similar traffic and 
roadway conditions? 
III. How do dynamic factors such as congestion level, time of day, and intersection type 
influence the PCU values of tricycles in Kampala, as determined by different estimation 
methods (headway ratio, multiple linear regression, dynamic PCU analysis)? 
1.5 Justification of the study 
Accurate estimation of PCU values for tricycles is essential for improving road design, traffic 
management, and sustainable mob ility in Kampala, Uganda. Tricycles have become a 
significant component of Kampala’s transport system, providing affordable and flexible 
mobility for thousands of commuters and entrepreneurs, especially in areas underserved by 
conventional public transport  (ChinAfrica, 2024; Okiza et al., 2024). However, their unique 
operational characteristics and lack of integration into existing traffic models have contributed 
to inefficiencies in urban traffic management and infrastructure planning (Baertsch, 2020).

1.6.2 Temporal Scope 
Data collection will be conducted during both the dry and wet seasons to capture seasonal 
variations in traffic patterns and tricycle operations. Observations will be made across multiple 
time periods, including morning and evening peak hours, as well as off-peak intervals, on both 
weekdays and weekends. This approach ensures that the resulting PCU values reflect the full 
range of traffic conditions encountered in Kampala (Okiza et al., 2024). 
1.6.3 Vehicle Scope and Justification 
The study will focus on both passenger and cargo tricycles operating within Kampala’s urban 
corridors. While passenger tricycles constitute the majority and are prominent contributors to 
mixed-traffic dynamics and congestion at key intersections, cargo tricycles also play a growing 
role in urban mobility and logistics. Including both types allows for a more comprehensive 
analysis of their collective impact on traffic flow, road safety, and intersection performance in 
the selected study areas (ChinAfrica, 2024; KCCA, 2023). 
1.6.4 Methodological Scope 
Video data will be captured using high -definition cameras (1080p, 60fps) mounted at a 
minimum height of 5.5m to minimise vehicle occlusion. Recording will be strictly limited to 
daylight hours (06:30 –18:30) to ensure classification accuracy.  To validate data quality, a 
random 15-minute sample from each hour will be cross -coded by two independent observers. 
Inter-rater reliability will be assessed using Cohen’s Kappa, with a minimum acceptance 
threshold of A kappa (𝜅) value of ≥ 0.8 shall be required before full analysis proceeds. 
The research will apply established PCU estimation methods  including the headway ratio, 
multiple linear regression, and dynamic PCU analysis across the selected sites and time periods. 
The study will also incorporate  statistical modelling to examine the influence of congestion, 
intersection type, and temporal factors on tricycle PCU values. 
1.6.5 Exclusions 
The study will not cover rural roads, peri-urban corridors with negligible tricycle activity. This 
ensures the research remains focused on the most relevant and impactful segment of Kampala’s 
urban mobility landscape. 
1.6.6 Representativeness and Relevance 
By selecting high -density corridors and major intersections and by capturing data across 
seasons and time periods, the stud y ensures that the derived PCU values are robust,

CHAPTER TWO 
2 LITERATURE REVIEW 
2.1 Introduction 
This chapter ent ails existing literature on Passenger Car Unit determination, focusing on 
methodologies used in its estimation, factors affecting PCU values, and previous studies on 
tricycle traffic behavior. 
Current literature reveals three critical gaps that this study addresses: 
i. Lack of Behavioral PCUs: No studies currently quantify the PCU effect of 'aggressive 
weaving' unique to Kampala tricycles. 
ii. Temporal Granularity: Existing PCU estimates are static and do not account for the severe 
congestion window (18:30–20:00) identified by JICA (2022). 
iii. Simulation Parameters: There are no validated calibration parameters (e.g., standstill 
distances) for tricycles in VISSIM models for Uganda.". 
2.2 Conceptual Review 
AA central concept in traffic engineering is the PCU, a metric used to standardize the diverse 
effects of various vehicle types on traffic flow and road capacity (Rahman et al., 2019). In 
heterogeneous urban environments like Kampala, where the traffic stream comprises a mix of 
motorcycles, tricycles, passenger cars, minib uses, and non -motorized vehicles, assigning 
accurate PCU values is crucial for quantifying each vehicle type’s impact and for effective road 
design, traffic management, and policy formulation (Baertsch, 2020; Olawale et al., 2017). 
PCU values are determine d by evaluating how a vehicle interacts with the traffic stream, 
considering factors such as maneuverability, size, speed, acceleration, and deceleration 
(Olawale et al., 2017). Larger vehicles, such as buses and trucks, typically have higher PCU 
values due to their greater road space occupancy and slower speeds, which disrupt traffic flow 
more significantly. Motorcycles, by contrast, have lower PCU values because of their small 
size and high maneuverability. Tricycles, however, present a unique challenge: their 
intermediate size, moderate speed, and distinctive movement behaviors —such as frequent 
stops and unpredictable lane usage—set them apart from both motorcycles and passenger cars, 
complicating PCU assessment (Olawale et al., 2017; Adams et al., 2014).

2.2.1 Unique Operational Characteristics of Tricycles in Kampala 
Tricycles in Kampala operate under mixed traffic conditions, often lacking designated lanes 
and contending for space with both smaller and larger vehicles (Baertsch, 2020). Their

2.2.1.1 Why Existing PCU Models Are Insufficient for Tricycles 
Most established PCU model s are based on the behaviors and characteristics of conventional 
vehicles cars, buses, and motorcycles—often under conditions of lane discipline and relatively 
homogeneous traffic (Rahman et al., 2019; Chandra & Sikdar, 2020). These models do not 
account for the unique operational dynamics of tricycles in Kampala, such as: 
i. Frequent passenger stops and starts, which disrupt flow more than continuous -moving 
motorcycles or cars. 
ii. Low lane discipline and frequent lateral movements, leading to unpredictable interactions 
with other vehicles. 
iii. Intermediate size and speed, making their impact on congestion context -dependent and 
highly variable. 
12 
 
iv. Variable passenger loads and operational patterns that differ from both motorcycles (lower 
load, higher maneuverability) and cars (higher speed, more consistent behavior). 
Empirical studies in similar African and Asian cities have shown that tricycle PCU values are 
consistently higher than those for motorcycles but lower than for cars, and can vary 
significantly by location, intersection type, and traffic density (Adams et al., 2014; Raj et al., 
2018; Okiza et al., 2024). For example, Adams et al. (2014) found PCU values for tricycles 
ranging from 0.67 to 0.75 at different intersections in Ghana, while Raj et al. (2018) observed 
values from 0.91 to 1.32 in Indian urban roads, underscoring the need for localized, context -
specific estimation. 
2.2.1.2 Implications for Kampala 
Accurate PCU estimation for tricycles is essential for: 
i. Improving road capacity analysis and intersection design by r eflecting the true impact of 
tricycles on flow and congestion. 
ii. Optimizing traffic signal timings and lane allocation to accommodate the operational 
realities of tricycles. 
iii. Supporting sustainable urban mobility planning, given the growing modal share of 
tricycles in Kampala’s transport system (Baertsch, 2020; ChinAfrica, 2024). 
Without Kampala -specific PCU values for tricycles, traffic models risk underestimating 
congestion and misallocating road space, leading to persistent inefficiencies and missed 
opportunities for targeted interventions (Okiza et al., 2024; Adams et al., 2014). 
2.3 Theoretical Review 
2.3.1 Traffic Flow and PCU Concept 
The characteristics of traffic flow vary between developed and developing nations. Cars and a 
few other vehicles make up the majorit y of the traffic flow in developed nations, which is 
homogeneous in nature and follows a straightforward and orderly pattern. However, traffic in 
developing nations is known as heterogeneous, and it includes a diverse range of vehicles with 
various static and dynamic features. For road projects to be planned and carried out effectively, 
traffic flow needs to be precisely measured (Chandra and Kumar, 2023). Given the diverse 
traffic conditions, it is inappropriate to express traffic flow in terms of the numb er of vehicles 
that pass a specific section in a given amount of time. When the effects of each type of vehicle 
are expressed in terms of some common units known as Passenger Car Units (PCU) or 
13 
 
Passenger Car Equivalents (PCE), the difficulties associated with describing the diverse traffic 
volume can be resolved. 
In traffic engineering, different types of vehicles share the same roadway, but their sizes, 
speeds, and maneuverability vary significantly. To ensure efficient traffic management and road 
capacity analysis, engineers use a standardized measure called the Passenger Car Unit (PCU). 
A Passenger Car Unit (PCU) is a measure of a vehicle's interaction with traffic compared to a 
standard passenger car under specific conditions (CSIR - Central Road Researc h Institute, 
2017). PCU is a metric that expresses the impact of a particular vehicle type on traffic flow 
relative to a standard passenger car. Since larger or slower vehicles take up more space and 
time on the road compared to smaller, faster ones, their PCU values differ. 
In traffic studies, the passenger car unit (PCU) is important. In the United States, the Highway 
Capacity Manual (HCM) coined the word "PCU" to refer to the volume or capacity of 
passenger automobiles per hour per lane (Lera and Kuleno,  2020). PCU was defined in the 
Highway Capacity Manual (Highway Research Board 1965) as "the number of passenger cars 
displaced in the traffic flow by a truck or a bus, under the existing roadway and traffic 
conditions" (Ameena and Dhurai, 2023). PCU was d efined as "the measure of relative 
interaction between a vehicle and a traffic stream with respect to a standard passenger car under 
a given set of highway and traffic conditions" by the Central Road Research Institute (CSIR) 
in 2017. It is used as a conversion factor to compare the impact of various vehicle types, such 
as motorcycles, tricycles, buses, and trucks, to that of a standard passenger car in terms of 
traffic flow and road capacity (Lera and Kuleno, 2020). The Passenger Car Unit (PCU) is a 
factor that converts mixed traffic into an equivalent number of passenger cars. It is also defined 
as the ratio of the time taken by a given type of vehicle to traverse a road segment compared to 
a standard passenger car under similar conditions (Khisty and Lall , 2016). PCU values help 
traffic engineers assess road capacity, determine lane requirements, and optimize traffic signals 
for smoother flow. For example, heavy trucks and buses have higher PCU values since they 
occupy more space and move at lower speeds, while motorcycles and bicycles have lower PCU 
values due to their smaller footprint and higher maneuverability (Stewart -Wilson and 
Mutabazi, 2017). 
PCU values are crucial for traffic analysis, capacity assessment, and roadway design. They help 
in estimating congestion levels, optimizing signal timings, and designing road infrastructure. 
The concept of PCU is crucial in traffic engineering as it facilitates traffic engineers in 
14 
 
standardizing heterogeneous traffic into homogeneous units, thus simplifying traffic modeling 
and simulation (Chandra and Sikdar, 2020).  
PCU concept allows for the evaluation of road capacity in diverse traffic conditions. Roads are 
typically designed based on the expected number of vehicles they can handle, but since traffic 
is rarely composed of just cars, PCU values provide a realistic way to account for mixed traffic. 
This ensures that infrastructure planning is based on actual usage rather than theoretical 
assumptions (Vermeiren et al., 2015). Also, PCU values play a vital role in traffic flow analysis 
and management. Engineers use PCU -based calculations to design and time traffic signals 
efficiently, ensuring that vehicles of different sizes receive appropriate green -light durations 
(Gwilliam, 2013). Without this adjustment, large vehicles could create unnecessary congestion, 
while smaller ones could be under -prioritized (Arnott et al ., 2015). PCU is essential for 
roadway design and lane allocation because by understanding how different vehicle types 
influence traffic movement, engineers can allocate lanes more effectively (Banskota and Shahi, 
2021). For instance, dedicated bus lanes or truck lanes can be planned based on the PCU values 
of these vehicles, ensuring smoother movement and reduced congestion. Additionally, PCU 
assists in traffic simulation and modeling, which helps researchers and policymakers predict 
traffic behavior under different scenarios. By incorporating PCU values into simulations, 
planners can test road designs, assess the impact of increased vehicle numbers, an d develop 
better traffic management strategies (Baertsch, 2020). 
2.3.2 Methods of PCU Estimation 
In order to manage traffic regulation and control, as well as to construct and evaluate the 
operation of various traffic facilities, it is essential to estimate PCU for each vehicle type in the 
traffic stream. Accurately measuring the homogenous traffic volume in mixed traffic scenarios 
is made possible by using the proper PCU for each vehicle category. This is crucial for 
delivering the required level of service. Fur thermore, a situation of unexpected congestion 
could arise from a poor implementation of the PCU. The PCU of distinct vehicle categories 
can be estimated using a variety of techniques (Pooja et al., 2019). 
The headway method, homogenous coefficient method,  walker's method, multiple linear 
regression method, simultaneous equations method, speed based method, huber method, and 
simulation technique are among the most popular methods for estimating PCU of a vehicle 
category (Biswas & Sharma, 2021).

as they pass a point on a roadway (Khisty and Lall, 2016). In this approach, the average 
headway (time interval between vehicles) of a specific vehicle type is measured and compared

space and time, leading to a higher PCU value. This approach is easy to use and performs best 
at higher service levels and with less mixed traffic. However, in crowded or extremely dynamic 
traffic scenarios when vehicle interactions vary greatly, its accuracy can be jeopardized.

instruments or methods. Obtaining sufficient samples from more widely dispersed automobiles

The modified headway method is a straightforward and more efficient methodology that takes 
into account the vehicle's speed, estimated area, and time headway (McCormick, 2013).  It is 
16 
 
an improved version of the traditional headway method. Due to the limitations of the headway 
method particularly in heterogeneous traffic conditions where vehicle  speeds, sizes, and 
acceleration patterns vary significantly, the modified headway method incorporates additional 
factors such as speed variability, vehicle type interactions, and lane utilization to provide more 
accurate PCU estimates (Lera and Kuleno, 20 20). The modified headway method refines the 
traditional approach by accounting for factors such as vehicle classification, speed differences, 
and the influence of larger or slower-moving vehicles on overall traffic flow. Instead of relying 
solely on time gaps between vehicles, this method integrates multiple parameters to develop a 
more realistic estimation of a vehicle’s impact on traffic movement (McCormick, 2013). This 
makes it particularly useful in urban environments where traffic density is high and vehicles 
frequently change lanes or adjust speeds. 
Key improvements over the traditional headway method (McCormick, 2013; Ahmed, 2020): 
i) Incorporation of Speed Factors: The modified method includes speed variability in 
determining PCU values. It acknowledge s that slower -moving vehicles create greater 
disruptions in traffic flow, leading to higher PCU values, while faster -moving vehicles 
contribute less to congestion. 
ii) Consideration of Lane Utilization: Unlike the traditional method, which assumes vehicles 
remain in a single lane, the modified approach takes into account lane-changing behavior. 
This is particularly relevant in mixed traffic scenarios where smaller vehicles, such as 
motorcycles and auto-rickshaws, weave between lanes. 
iii) Adjustment for Vehicle Inte raction Effects: The method accounts for the influence of 
different vehicle types on each other ’s movement. For instance, heavy trucks and buses 
may force surrounding vehicles to slow down, thereby affecting the overall traffic flow. 
iv) Dynamic Headway Measur ement: Instead of using a fixed time interval for headway 
calculation, the modified method dynamically adjusts for variations in traffic density and 
congestion levels, providing more adaptable PCU values. 
The modified headway method is widely used in studi es where accurate PCU estimation is 
necessary for better traffic modeling and infrastructure planning ( Ahmed, 2020). The process 
begins with data collection, where traffic flow data, including headway times, vehicle speeds, 
and lane utilization patterns, are recorded using sensors, video analysis, or manual observation. 
Once the data is collected, vehicles are classified based on their size, maneuverability, and 
speed characteristics to ensure a more accurate estimation of their impact on traffic flow. 
After classification, the calculation of dynamic headway ratios is performed. This involves 
comparing the time taken by a particular vehicle type to traverse a road section with that of a 
17 
 
passenger car, while adjusting for factors such as speed, lane changes, and congestion levels. 
Finally, the PCU values for each vehicle category are derived using modified equations that 
incorporate these additional parameters, ensuring a more precise estimation. 
One of the key advantages of the modified headway method is its higher accuracy in mixed 
traffic conditions. By including multiple factors such as speed variations and vehicle 
interactions, this method results in a more precise estimation of PCU values ( Ahmed, 2020). 
Another significant advantage is its ability to better represent real-world traffic behavior. Since 
the method takes into account lane utilization and speed variations, it provides a more practical 
analysis of how different vehicle types impact road capacity (Pooja et al., 2019). In addition, 
the modified h eadway method improves infrastructure planning by providing reliable PCU 
estimates. Traffic engineers can use these estimates to design roadways, intersections, and 
signal systems that better accommodate actual traffic conditions, leading to more efficient  
transportation networks (Ogundipe, 2018). Furthermore, the method's adaptability makes it 
suitable for different traffic environments. It can be applied in both urban and rural settings, 
making it a versatile tool for traffic analysis and management.  
Despite its advantages, the modified headway method also has certain challenges (Ogundipe, 
2018; Ahmed, 2020; Pooja et al., 2019): 
i) One major challenge is the complexity in data collection. The method requires extensive 
traffic monitoring and real -time data an alysis, which can make its implementation 
resource-intensive.  
ii) Variability in traffic conditions can affect the accuracy of PCU estimation. Differences in 
road design, driver behavior, and vehicle characteristics may require location -specific 
adjustments to ensure reliable results. 
iii) Another limitation is the computational demand associated with this method. Since the 
modified headway method involves advanced data processing and statistical modeling, it 
may pose challenges in regions with limited technical re sources. The requirement for 
specialized software and expertise in traffic data analysis can also make the application of 
this method more demanding.

2.3.3 Estimated Value of PCU 
In traffic engineering, the PCU is a crucial metric used to express the impact of different vehicle 
types on traffic flow in relation to a standard passenger car. Since vehicles vary in size, speed, 
and maneuverability, PCU values help standardize their effect on road capacity and congestion 
levels. The estimated value of PCU can be determined using two primary approaches: Static 
PCU values and Dynamic PCU values. Each approach provides different insights into how 
various vehicle types influence traffic movement. 
26 
 
Static PCU values assign a fixed equivalency factor to each vehicle type, representing its 
impact on traffic flow relative to a single passenger car under uniform, controlled conditions. 
These values, such as those in the Highway Capacity Manual or Indonesian Highway Capacity 
Manual, are based on average vehicle dimensions and operational characteristics, assuming 
steady traffic with minimal variation. While static PCU values are straightforward and  useful 
for infrastructure planning in consistent environments, they do not account for real -world 
variability in traffic, potentially leading to inaccuracies in capacity assessments when actual 
conditions differ from the assumed standards. 
Dynamic PCU values, in contrast, reflect the fluctuating impact of different vehicle types under 
varying traffic conditions, taking into account factors like speed, congestion, lane discipline,  
and road geometry. This method uses empirical data, simulations, or regressio n models to 
estimate how vehicles interact in heterogeneous and unpredictable traffic, providing more 
accurate and adaptive measures for traffic management and control. However, dynamic PCU 
estimation is complex and data -intensive, requiring advanced monit oring and analysis to 
capture the influence of numerous variables, including human behavior and roadside frictions, 
making it more resource-demanding than the static approach. 
This study prioritizes dynamic PCU estimation for tricycles in Kampala, Uganda, to account 
for the city’s highly variable traffic conditions, characterized by: 
i. Unpredictable congestion patterns: Peak -hour traffic density fluctuates by 40–70% on 
major corridors like Kampala Road and Jinja Road (Okiza et al., 2024; KCCA, 2023). 
ii. Heterogeneous interactions: Tricycles exhibit lane indiscipline, frequent stops, and 
variable speeds (20–35 km/h) that disrupt flow unpredictably (Baertsch, 2020; ChinAfrica, 
2024). 
iii. Context-dependent impacts: Tricycle PCU values vary significantly by intersection type 
(e.g., 0.91 at signalized junctions vs. 1.32 at roundabouts in similar cities) (Raj et al., 2018; 
Adams et al., 2014). 
2.3.3.1 Justification for Dynamic PCU Prioritization 
Traffic Variability: Kampala’s traffic operates without lane discipline, and tricycles adapt 
their behavior to avoid congestion, rendering static PCU values inadequate (Biswas & Sharma, 
2021; Dhamaniya & Chandra, 2016). 
27 
 
Operational Realities: Tricycles’ acceleration/deceleration rates (1.5–2.5 m/s²) and frequent 
stops increase their impact d uring peak hours, which static PCU models fail to capture 
(Alecsandru et al., 2023). 
Policy Relevance: Dynamic PCU aligns with Kampala Capital City Authority’s (KCCA) goal 
of adaptive traffic management, enabling time -sensitive interventions like peak -hour lane 
allocations (KCCA, 2022). 
2.3.3.2 Integration of Static PCU 
While dynamic PCU is prioritized, static PCU values will be calculated using the speed-area 
ratio method (Eq. 8) to: 
i. Provide baseline comparisons with IRC guidelines and regional studies (e.g., Nige ria’s 
static tricycle PCU = 1.8–2.0) (Olawole & Olakunle, 2022). 
ii. Support infrastructure planning for non -peak scenarios (e.g., rural -urban corridors with 
stable traffic).

Urban centers in developing countries are experien cing rapid increases in traffic volumes,

2.4.1 The Role and Characteristics of Tricycles in Kampala 
Tricycles, commonly known as tuk -tuks, are three -wheeled vehicles with two wheels at the 
back and one at the front, typically carrying three to four passengers (Oxford Press, 2017; 
ChinAfrica, 2024). In Kampala, tricycles have become a vital component of the urban transport 
system, particularly for first- and last-mile connectivity in areas underserved by formal public 
transport. Their affordability, ability to navigate congested and narrow streets, and flexible 
routing make them a preferred alternative for many commuters (Adebayo & Aluko, 2020; 
Behrens et al., 2016). 
Recent reports indicate that thousands of tricycles operate daily in Kampala, serving both 
passenger and cargo needs, with typical trip lengths ranging from 3 to 10 kilometers within the 
city (ChinAfrica, 2024). Tricycles are especially prevalent in densely populated neighborhoods 
and peri-urban areas, where they provide essential access to markets, schools, and workplaces. 
Their operational model is characterized by frequent stops, flexible routes, and informal 
scheduling, which, while beneficial f or users, can contribute to traffic flow disruptions and 
congestion (Okiza et al., 2024). 
2.4.1.1 Kampala-Specific Data and Operational Challenges 
i. Ridership: While precise figures are unavailable, anecdotal evidence and local authority 
estimates suggest that tricycles account for a significant share of informal public transport 
trips, particularly in areas lacking minibus or bus coverage (ChinAfrica, 2024). 
ii. Trip Lengths: Most tricycle journeys in Kampala are short -haul, typically under 10 km, 
catering to intra-city mobility and complementing longer-distance minibus or bus routes. 
iii. Operational Challenges: Tricycles in Kampala face several challenges, including poor 
road conditions, lack of designated parking or staging areas, and limited formal regulation. 
Their frequent stops, unpredictable lane changes, and tendency to operate outside formal 
traffic rules contribute to congestion and safety risks (Okiza et al., 2024; Baertsch, 2020). 
Additionally, the absence of standardized fare structures and weak enforcement of 
licensing requirements have led to inconsistent service quality and operational 
inefficiencies.

2.4.2 Comparative Perspective: Kampala vs. Other African Cities 
Kampala’s tricycle system shares similarities with those in other African cities such as Lagos 
(Nigeria) and Nairobi (Kenya), where tricycles have emerged as crucial modes of transport for 
low-income and peri -urban populations (Nwachukwu & Onu, 2019; Behrens et al., 2016). 
However, several unique features and regulatory differences distinguish Kampala’s context: 
i. Regulatory Environment: While cities like Lagos have implemented strict route 
restrictions and licensing regimes for tricycles, enforcement in Kampala remains limited, 
with tricycles operating in a largely informal environment (Ogunlana & Fakorede, 20 21; 
Okiza et al., 2024). Kampala authorities have debated bans or restrictions, but tricycles 
remain widely used due to public demand and lack of alternatives (Daily Monitor, 2017). 
ii. Integration with Formal Transport: In contrast to some Nigerian and Kenyan cities, where 
tricycles are increasingly integrated as feeder services to major transit hubs, Kampala ’s 
tricycles operate with minimal coordination with formal public transport, often filling 
service gaps in an ad hoc manner (Olvera et al., 2016; Behrens et al., 2016). 
iii. Socio-Economic Impact: Tricycles provide critical employment opportunities for drivers 
and support staff in Kampala, mirroring trends in other African cities. They also foster 
small-scale entrepreneurship, with some operators using tricycles for goods delivery, water 
transport, or even as makeshift ambulances in rural areas (ChinAfrica, 2024).

2.4.3 Impacts and Challenges of Tricycle 
While tricycles provide significant benefits, their presence in urban traffic raises several 
concerns. One of the primary issues is their contribution to congestion. Due to th eir relatively 
low speed and frequent stops, tricycles can disrupt the smooth flow of traffic, particularly in 
high-density areas (Eze & Okafor, 2021). Furthermore, they often operate outside formal traffic 
regulations, leading to increased road conflicts and higher accident risks (Afolabi et al., 2020). 
Another challenge associated with tricycles is their impact on road capacity. Studies have 
shown that tricycles have a Passenger Car Unit (PCU) value higher than motorcycles but lower 
than cars, affecting lane efficiency in mixed-traffic conditions (Olawole and Olakunle, 2022). 
Their unpredictable movement patterns and frequent lane switching contribute to increased 
travel time and reduced road efficiency (Chukwuma and Adewale, 2019). 
Many urban areas strugg le with enforcing traffic regulations for tricycles. In some cities, 
licensing and operational guidelines exist, but compliance is often poor due to weak 
enforcement mechanisms (Adeyemi & Ojo, 2020). For example, in Lagos, Nigeria, regulations 
require tricycle operators to follow designated routes, yet enforcement remains inconsistent 
(Ogunlana & Fakorede, 2021). The lack of standardized policies and poor rider training further 
exacerbate safety concerns. Some urban planners have attempted to regulate tricycle operations 
by restricting their movement on major roads and highways. However, such restrictions have 
been met with resistance, as tricycles remain an essential transport mode for many residents, 
particularly in low-income areas where alternative options are limited (Okonkwo et al., 2022) 
2.5 Gaps in Literature 
Despite the increasing presence of tricycles in urban transportation, particularly in cities like 
Kampala, there remain significant gaps in the literature regarding their Passenger Car Unit 
(PCU) values.  
2.5.1 Limited Research on Tricycle Traffic Behavior in Kampala 
Most existing studies on PCU values focus on conventional vehicle types such as cars, 
motorcycles, and heavy vehicles, with little attention given to tricycles in Kampala ’s unique 
mixed-traffic environment. There is a lack of research analyzing tricycle -specific movement 
patterns, acceleration profiles, and lane usage in the city. 
This research will collect empirical data at key intersections and corridors in Kampala (e.g., 
Wandegeya, Kibuye, Ba kuli) to systematically analyze tricycle movement patterns, 
31 
 
acceleration, deceleration, and lane usage. The study will document unique operational 
behaviors—such as frequent stops for passengers and high maneuverability —using direct 
observation and video analysis. 
2.5.2 Absence of Location-Specific PCU Estimations 
Many PCU studies adopt generalized values from international or regional models, which do 
not account for Kampala ’s distinctive road network, driver behavior, and traffic density. The 
lack of locally calibrated PCU values for tricycles results in inaccurate traffic flow predictions 
and suboptimal urban transport policies. 
The research will generate Kampala-specific PCU values for tricycles by analyzing local traffic 
conditions and vehicle interactions. T hese values will be validated against field data and 
contrasted with PCU values from other cities to highlight contextual differences and improve 
the accuracy of traffic modeling for Kampala. 
2.5.3 Inadequate Consideration of Traffic Density and Congestion Levels 
Existing PCU models often assume ideal or uniform traffic conditions, failing to account for 
the significant fluctuations in congestion and density seen in Kampala. In reality, PCU values 
are dynamic and vary with road capacity, peak and off-peak hours, and congestion levels. 
This study will estimate PCU values for tricycles under varying congestion scenarios by 
collecting data during both peak and off-peak hours, and across different seasons. The analysis 
will explicitly examine how tricycle PCU values c hange with traffic density and congestion, 
providing a more nuanced understanding relevant to Kampala’s conditions. 
2.5.4 Limited Use of Advanced Traffic Simulation Models 
Globally, advanced micro -simulation software (e.g., VISSIM, SIDRA, AIMSUN) is 
increasingly used to estimate PCU values, but such approaches are rarely applied to tricycles 
in Kampala. Reliance on traditional estimation methods may not fully capture the dynamic and 
heterogeneous nature of local traffic. 
The research will employ both traditional (headway ratio, regression) and advanced simulation 
techniques to estimate tricycle PCU values. Micro -simulation tools will be calibrated using 
empirical data from Kampala, allowing for a robust comparison of methods and a more 
comprehensive understanding of tricycle impacts.

2.5.5 Lack of Integration of Tricycles into Kampala’s Transport Policy 
Despite their widespread use, tricycles are not formally integrated into Kampala ’s 
transportation planning models, and there is minimal research on how assigning accurate PCU 
values could enhance infrastructure planning, lane allocation, or signal optimization. 
The study will explicitly evaluate the implications of integrating tricycle PCU values into 
Kampala’s transport policy and planning frameworks. It will provide acti onable 
recommendations for policymakers and urban planners on road design, intersection 
management, and traffic signal settings, supported by empirical data. 
2.5.6 Need for Empirical Data Collection and Validation 
Most studies estimating PCU values rely on theor etical assumptions or data from other 
contexts, rather than direct field observations in Kampala. 
This research will conduct comprehensive empirical data collection—including video analysis, 
manual counts, and automatic sensors —at representative sites in K ampala. The findings will 
be validated using statistical techniques and compared with regional and international 
benchmarks, ensuring reliability and local relevance.

2.7 Implications for Policy and Practice in Kampala 
Drawing on the reviewed evidence , this subsection outlines potential policy and design 
implications for Kampala’s tricycle system. 
2.7.1 Strategies for Effective Integration of Tricycles into Urban Transport Systems 
To maximize the benefits of tricycles while mitigating their negative impacts,  urban transport 
authorities must adopt context-sensitive strategies for their effective integration, especially in 
cities like Kampala where infrastructure and regulatory challenges are distinct. 
2.7.1.1 Designation of Exclusive Lanes for Tricycles 
For Kampala, preliminary feasibility checks indicate that citywide implementation of dedicated 
tricycle lanes is not currently realistic due to narrow right‑of‑way on many arterials, competing 
demand for NMT space, and significant on‑street trading activities (KCCA, 2023; JICA, 2010). 
Candidate pilot corridors will therefore be restricted to sections where:  
(i) Effective carriageway width after accounting for NMT and parking is at least 10.5 m;  
37 
 
(ii) No major road rehabilitation works are planned during the study period; and  
(iii) Enforcement presence by Kampala Metropolitan Police can be ensured to maintain 
lane discipline. These criteria will guide the selection of any pilot corridors tested 
using the calibrated PCU and simulation results. 
2.7.1.2 Improvement of the Regulatory Framework 
Kampala faces persistent challenges in enforcing licensing, training, and operational guidelines 
for tricycle operators (Adeyemi & Ojo, 2020; Okiza et al., 2024). Strengthening the regulatory 
framework should include periodic road safety training, mandatory o perator licensing, and 
targeted enforcement at congestion -prone junctions. The Kampala Capital City Authority 
(KCCA) has begun to pilot digital registration and operator training schemes, but coverage 
remains limited (KCCA, 2023). Enhanced collaboration wi th tricycle associations and 
community leaders can improve compliance and foster safer driving practices (Ekezie & Umeh, 
2023). 
2.7.1.3 Integration into Formal Public Transport Networks 
Tricycles in Kampala currently operate largely informally, with minimal coordi nation with 
formal public transport (Okeke et al., 2022; Stewart -Wilson & Mutabazi, 2017). Urban 
planners should pilot the integration of tricycles as official feeder services to major taxi parks 
and BRT (Bus Rapid Transit) corridors, especially in peri-urban areas where minibus coverage 
is sparse (JICA, 2010). Such integration could involve designated pick -up/drop-off points at 
transport hubs and the use of digital platforms to coordinate services, as seen in pilot initiatives 
in Nairobi and Lagos. 
In the empirical component of this study, integration will be operationalized through 
measurable indicators at selected junctions and hubs, including:  
(i) Average transfer walking distance between tricycle drop‑off points and formal public 
transport boarding points;  
(ii) Average tricycle dwell time at designated and undesignated stages. 
(iii) Observed queuing and blocking events of formal vehicles by tricycles. Improvements 
in these indicators under hypothetical operational changes (e.g., consolidated staging 
points) will be tested via simulation as part of the policy implications 
2.7.1.4 Investment in Modern, Environmentally Friendly Tricycle Models 
Transitioning to cleaner, more efficient tricycle models can help reduce emissions and improve 
air quality in Kampala (Adekunle et al., 2 021). Given the city ’s air pollution challenges and 
38 
 
the government’s interest in sustainable urban mobility, pilot programs for electric or low -
emission tricycles could be launched in partnership with local manufacturers and international 
donors. These pil ots should be accompanied by incentives for operators to upgrade vehicles 
and by the development of charging/refueling infrastructure.

3.2 Research Design 
This study will employ a quantitative research design, combining field data collection, 
statistical analysis, and comparative evaluation to determine the PCU  values for tricycles in 
Kampala City, Uganda.  The research will begin with the selection of representative road 
segments and intersections within the city where tricycles are commonly used. Traffic data  
including vehicle type, speed, headway, and traffic volume, will be collected using video 
cameras and manual counting during peak and off-peak hours to capture a comprehensive range 
of traffic conditions. The data will then be categorized based on vehicle  types; tricycles 
(classified by size), motorcycles, cars, and other relevant modes of transport. The study will 
apply established PCU estimation methods, including the headway ratio method, multiple 
linear regression analysis, and dynamic PCU estimation, to calculate PCU values for each 
41 
 
tricycle category. Statistical software will be used to perform regression modeling and evaluate 
the influence of traffic and roadway parameters on PCU values. The resulting PCU values will 
be compared with existing values from similar urban settings to assess contextual differences 
and validate the reliability of the findings. This systematic approach will ensure a robust and 
context-specific determination of PCU values that can improve the accuracy of traffic flow 
modeling and planning in Kampala City. 
For the sampling strategy, data will be collected over a period of 20 consecutive days, including 
both weekdays and weekends, to capture temporal variations in traffic flow (Okiza et al., 2024). 
Each study site will be observed for a minimum of four hours per day, split between morning 
peak (6:30–9:30 a.m.), midday off-peak (11:00 a.m.–1:00 p.m.), and evening peak (4:00–7:00 
p.m.) periods. This distribution ensures that both peak and off -peak conditions are adequately 
represented and allows for the analysis of diurnal traffic patterns (JICA, 2010). Key 
intersections and corridors Wandegeya, Bwaise, Bakuli, Natete, and Kibuye  will be sampled 
on a rotating schedule, with at least three non -consecutive observation days at each loc ation. 
This approach controls for day -of-week effects and ensures that no single location 
disproportionately influences the results. The selection of sites is based on documented high 
tricycle density and congestion (KCCA, 2023; JICA, 2010). Data collection will occur during 
both dry and wet seasons to account for seasonal variability in traffic behavior (Okiza et al., 
2024). Observations will be paused or annotated during extraordinary events (e.g., major

3.4 Data Collection 
In the study, a detailed and multi -instrumental approach will be employed to ensure 
comprehensive and reliable data collection under mixed traffic conditions. High‑definition 
video cameras (minimum 1080p resolution at 30–60 frames per second) will be mounted on 
existing roadside structures (e.g., lighting poles, building facades) at heights of 5.0–6.0 m to 
minimise occlusion from larger vehicles. Camera positioning will ensure a clear field of view 
covering at least 100 m of eac h approach and the entire intersection area. Recording will be 
restricted to daylight hours between 06:30 and 18:30 to support reliable manual classification. 
To manage occlusion, overlapping camera coverage will be used at complex junctions (e.g., 
Kibuye) and video segments with seve re occlusion will be flagged and excluded from PCU 
estimation. Inter‑observer reliability will be assessed by having at least 20% of the video 
segments independently coded by two trained enumerators. A minimum Cohen ’s kappa 
coefficient of 0.8 will be required for acceptance; if this threshold is not met, additional training 
and re‑coding will be undertaken 
Vehicles will be classified into tricycles (by size and operational class), motorcycles, cars, and 
other relevant categories. Special attention will be g iven to distinguishing passenger tricycles  
from cargo tricycles, with the latter excluded from the analysis to align with the study ’s focus 
on urban passenger mobility. To ensure accurate differentiation of tricycles from motorcycles 
and small cars in Kamp ala’s mixed traffic, a multi -layered approach combining manual and 
automated methods will be employed, with rigorous quality assurance protocols. 
3.4.1 Tricycle Identification Criteria 
3.4.1.1 Physical Characteristics (Video Analysis): 
i. Structural Features: Tricycles in Kampala are distinctively three -wheeled, with a sidecar 
attached to a motorcycle base. Manual enumerators will be trained to identify this 
43 
 
configuration, which differs from motorcycles (two wheels) and small cars (four wheels) 
(ChinAfrica, 2024; KCCA, 2023). 
ii. Dimensions: Tricycles typically measure 2.6–3.2 meters in length and 1.3–1.5 meters in 
width, larger than motorcycles (1.8–2.2 meters) but smaller than compact cars (3.5–4.5 
meters) (Okiza et al., 2024). 
iii. Color and Markings: Many Kampala tricycles featur e standardized color schemes (e.g., 
yellow bodies with black stripes) and registration plates, aiding visual identification 
(KCCA, 2022). 
3.4.1.2 Automated Classification: 
i. Axle Count and Length: Pneumatic tube counters and induction loops will classify vehicles 
based on axle count (tricycles: 3 axles; motorcycles: 2 axles) and length thresholds 
(tricycles: 2.6–3.2m) (Icoms Detections, 2024). 
ii. Speed Profiles: Tricycles in Kampala average 20–35 km/h, slower than motorcycles (30–
50 km/h) but comparable to cars in congested conditions (Baertsch, 2020). 
3.4.2 Enumerator Training and Reliability Checks 
3.4.2.1 Training Program 
Enumerators will undergo a 5-day training program covering: 
i. Image Recognition: Reviewing photo/video examples of Kampala-specific tricycle models 
(e.g., Bajaj RE, TVS King). 
ii. Field Practice: Conducting supervised counts at pilot sites (Wandegeya, Kibuye) to refine 
classification accuracy. 
iii. Software Use: Training on video analysis tools (e.g., Tracker Video Analysis) for frame -
by-frame review. 
3.4.2.2 Inter-Observer Reliability: 
i. Double-Blind Reviews: 20% of video footage will be independently analyzed by two 
enumerators. Consistency will be measured using Cohen’s kappa (κ), with a target κ ≥ 0.85 
(Science.gov, 2018). 
ii. Weekly Audits: Supervisors will cross -check 10% of manual co unts against automated 
data, resolving discrepancies through consensus. 
44 
 
3.4.3 Data Quality Assurance 
3.4.3.1 Cross-Validation: 
i. Triangulation: Manual counts from video analysis will be compared with automated sensor 
data hourly. Discrepancies >5% will trigger re-analysis. 
ii. Peak-Hour Focus: Additional enumerators will be deployed during peak periods (7–9 a.m., 
4–7 p.m.) to handle high-density traffic and reduce miscounts. 
3.4.3.2 Equipment Calibration: 
i. Daily Checks: Pneumatic tubes and induction loops will be calibrated each morning using 
known vehicle samples (e.g., pre-measured tricycles). 
ii. Weather Mitigation: Cameras will use infrared filters during rain, and data from severely 
adverse weather will be flagged/excluded (FHWA, 2004). 
3.4.3.3 Error Handling: 
i. Ambiguous Cases: Blurry or occlud ed vehicles in video footage will be categorized as 
"unclassified" and excluded from final analysis (<2% target). 
ii. Sensor Failures: Redundant sensors will be installed at each site, with data gaps filled via 
linear interpolation from adjacent time intervals.

3.6 Research Ethics and Data Privacy 
This study will adhere to the highest ethical standards in the collection, storage, and use of data. 
Video recordings and manual observations will be conducted exclusively in public spaces, with 
no attempt to identify individuals. All data will be anonymized, and footage will be stored 
securely with access limited to the research team. Where required, permissions will be sought 
from the KCCA and relevant local authorities. Enumerators will be trained on ethical conduct, 
including non -interference in traffic and respect for privacy. The research protocol will be 
submitted for review and clearanc e by an accredited institutional ethics committee prior to 
fieldwork.  
Prior to installation of cameras, formal permission will be obtained from KCCA and local 
division authorities. Community leaders (LC1) at each site will be informed about the purpose, 
duration and nature of the video recordings, and public notices stating ‘Traffic Survey in 
Progress. No facial identification ’ will be displayed at visible locations. Although recordings 
occur in public right‑of‑way, individuals who express concerns or objections to being filmed 
will be informed that only aggregate, anonymised traffic data (vehicle counts and categories) 
will be retained and th at no attempt will be made to identify drivers or pedestrians. Contact 
details of the principal investigator and REC will be provided on the notices for any further 
queries or complaints. 
To prevent third‑party misuse, raw video files will be stored on enc rypted drives on a secure 
server with access limited to the principal investigator and a designated data manager. No raw 
video will be shared with external agencies, including law enforcement, and no screenshots or 
clips showing identifiable faces or licen se plates will appear in reports or presentations. Only 
aggregated traffic indicators (e.g., classified counts, headway distributions, PCU estimates) 
will be disseminated. Access logs will be maintained to record every instance of file access and 
ensure auditability. 
Raw video data will be retained for a maximum of five years after completion of the study to 
allow for verification and potential secondary analysis. After this period, all raw footage will 
be permanently deleted using secure deletion protocols, while anonymised, aggregated datasets 
and codebooks will be archived for longer‑term research use. Access to raw video will be 
restricted to the principal investigator and one authorised data manager, whereas anonymised 
derived datasets may be shared wit h supervisors and collaborators under data‑sharing 
agreements. Compliance with th   ese retention and access rules will be monitored via annual  
49 
 
checks of the storage system and a simple access log which records user, date, and purpose of 
each access event. 
3.7 Risk Assessment and Mitigation 
Potential risks to project success include equipment malfunction (e.g., camera or sensor 
failure), adverse weather affecting data quality, observer bias, and disruptions from public 
events or roadworks. To mitigate these ris ks, the study will deploy redundant data collection 
equipment at each site, schedule flexible observation periods to accommodate weather or 
unforeseen disruptions, and implement rigorous enumerator training and cross -validation. All 
deviations from the dat a collection plan will be documented, and contingency days are built 
into the timeline to ensure data completeness. Regular supervisory audits will ensure data 
integrity and adherence to protocols. 
Field safety risks will be minimised by coordinating insta llation times with KCCA and 
Kampala Metropolitan Police to avoid obstructing live traffic. Enumerators and technicians 
will wear high‑visibility clothing and operate only from safe locations (sidewalks, medians or 
adjacent buildings), without standing in live lanes. Warning cones and temporary signage will 
be deployed where necessary during installation. No data collection will be undertaken during 
extreme weather or in situations where political rallies, protests or other events compromise 
safety; such periods will be documented and alternative days scheduled.
